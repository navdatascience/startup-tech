Mimi: Welcome to Startup Tech. I am Mimi, here with Alex, and we start today with a number that sounds made up. One solo founder, no employees, no investors, running more than twenty software products at once, bringing in about two hundred and fifty thousand dollars a month. That is John Rush on Starter Story, and his system for doing it is the best idea of the day.
Alex: Two hundred and fifty thousand dollars a month, from twenty plus apps, by himself. My first instinct is that nobody can actually operate that many products. So how is this not just chaos with good marketing?
Mimi: That is exactly the right question, and Rush answers it with structure. Across his portfolio he reports about three million dollars a year in revenue and about one million users. But here is the honest part. Only about seven of the products bring in most of the revenue. The rest are not failures. They are audience machines that feed customers into the paid products. Alex: So the portfolio is really a funnel. The small tools gather founders, and the bigger tools collect the money. He is not running twenty businesses. He is running one audience with twenty doors into it.
Mimi: Exactly, and his building process is just as disciplined. For every idea he follows the same order. Find a real pain first. Build a waitlist. Pre-sell the product before it exists. Deliver the first version manually, by hand. Only after people have paid does he write the serious code.
Alex: I love that order because it kills the most common founder mistake, building for three months and then discovering nobody wanted it. If nobody pre-pays, the idea dies in a week, not a quarter. And he does not build everything alone either, right?
Mimi: Right. He uses what he calls co-makers, partners who help build and run individual products, and then he cross-promotes across the whole portfolio, so every launch starts with an audience instead of starting from zero. Alex: Underrated is an interesting word choice. Everyone fights over the single smartest model, but for a business, the model that finishes a whole task quickly, without stalling or wandering off, might matter more than the one that wins a benchmark by a point or two.
Mimi: That is the business angle. So let us turn it into an idea, the way Greg likes to do. Who buys? Operators and small business owners who have one repetitive end-to-end job, like turning raw leads into researched outreach, or turning support tickets into drafted replies and updated records. What is version one? You do not build a platform. You wire one workflow, for one customer, on the fast model, and you deliver the finished output, not a dashboard they have to learn.
Alex: And pricing follows the value, not the tokens. A setup fee to build the workflow, then a monthly retainer to run and improve it. The model being fast and cheaper to run is what protects your margin. If the workflow saves a team ten hours a week, charging for that outcome is an easy conversation. If you charge by the hour, you just made yourself the bottleneck.
