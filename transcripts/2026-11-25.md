A founder in Chicago is on a mission to launch a new one million dollar business every single month, twelve of them in twelve months, and he is not starting each one from scratch. He starts each one from the same productized service engine, so the selling, the delivery, and the cash flow already work before the new business even has a name. That is the story Starter Story flew to Chicago to hear, and it changes how you should think about launching.
Alex: I am Alex, and I love that as an opening because it flips the usual advice. Most founders are told to focus on one thing for ten years. This founder built one repeatable machine, and now the machine launches the businesses. Today we also have an encrypted app built in three weeks that went from sixty six dollars in its first month to about two thousand dollars a day, a war breaking out over whether artificial intelligence agents are allowed to buy things for you, and a company with zero employees claiming a ten million dollar run rate. Let us start with Starter Story.
Max: The founder is Hunter Hammonds, and the Starter Story video is called He is Launching a New One Million Dollar Business Every Month. The method, in his own telling, is that a productized service acts as the launchpad for a much bigger vision. A productized service means the offer is packaged in advance. The buyer knows what they get, what it costs, and how fast it arrives, and the founder knows exactly how to deliver it because he has delivered it before.
Alex: And that packaging is why the pace is even possible. When every new business begins with a service that already produces revenue, you are not waiting for a product to find its market. You are using real customer money and real customer feedback from week one, and then you decide which of those services deserves to become something bigger. His earlier design subscription business, Off Menu, grew to millions in revenue in months on exactly this model, a premium subscription for design and branding instead of slow, custom, one off projects.
Max: So the playbook for a listener is smaller and more honest than launching twelve businesses. Launch one packaged service first. Give it one buyer, one promise, and one fixed way of delivering it. Run it until the delivery feels boring, because boring delivery is what frees you to launch the next thing. If the service needs a brand new pitch and a brand new process for every customer, you do not have a launchpad yet. You have a job.
Alex: Starter Story also gave us a second story that rhymes beautifully, and this one is about patience. Rasty Turek built Vaultaire, an iPhone app that stores files and photos in encrypted vaults, right on the device, with no servers and no shared keys. It was his first iPhone app, he built it in about three weeks using artificial intelligence coding agents, and in its first month it made just sixty six dollars.
Max: Sixty six dollars. Most people would have quit and called it a failed experiment. Six months later, the app made forty one thousand dollars in a single month, and by the time of the interview it was averaging around two thousand to three thousand dollars a day, with more than sixty thousand downloads. And here is the detail I want you to remember. His conversion curve runs backwards compared with most apps. Most apps convert best on day one. His app converts worst on day one, at about five percent, and best around day sixty, because people only pay once they have trusted the vault with things that actually matter to them.
Alex: And his growth channel was not social media and it was not paid ads. It was search, run almost entirely by agents. His system finds the questions people are already asking, then publishes about one helpful guide a day, linked together so the strong pages lift the new ones. One guide a day sounds small, until you multiply it by six months of showing up. The lesson across both Starter Story stories is the same. A repeatable engine, run daily, beats a brilliant launch almost every time.
Max: Now to Greg Isenberg, and a fight he says will decide how we all buy things for the next ten years. His episode is called The Nine Stages of Artificial Intelligence Agent Adoption, and he calls the fight the war on agents. The setup is simple. Big companies spent two years telling us agents would do everything for us. Now some of those same companies are blocking outside agents from their stores.
Alex: Walk through the three camps, because this is the part a builder can actually use. In the blocking camp, Amazon has blocked Meta's personal agent from browsing and buying in its store, one major airline says it works with zero outside agents for booking and treats them as unauthorized activity, and a big reviews platform says agents are welcome only if they pay for access. In the middle camp, companies say agents acting for real customers are welcome, but their anti scraping tools block those agents by accident. And in the welcoming camp, the agent platforms and the payment networks are opening the doors on purpose.
Max: Greg's best line in the episode reframes the whole thing. An agent is a customer with no eyes and a credit card. It does not see your homepage video, your banner, or your countdown timer. It reads your prices, your shipping costs, and your return policy, and it stays loyal to whatever works. So companies block agents mainly where the money is fattest, the ads an agent skips and the upgrades an agent refuses, and the deeper fear is that someone else ends up owning the customer relationship.
Alex: So here is the buildable idea, in Greg's style. Pick one niche where buying is painful and repetitive, say local service businesses that need supplies every week. Your first version is an agent readiness service done by hand. You test whether an agent can find the business, understand its prices, and complete an order, and you fix everything that breaks, ten times, for ten real businesses. Who buys it? The owner who is about to lose sales to a competitor the agent can actually read. Price it as a setup fee for the fix, plus a monthly fee to keep the business readable and bookable as the platforms change. Greg argues a trusted middle layer is coming, the same way it came for bank data years ago, and the founders who did the work by hand first will own it.
Max: Now our third source, The Next New Thing, where Andrew Warner pushed a founder harder than usual, and the numbers got interesting. The company is Polsia, a platform that claims any non technical person can launch and run a startup using its agents, which build, market, and operate the business. Andrew asked Ben Cera to show the real data, the revenue, the churn, and the infrastructure costs, instead of just the demo.
Alex: And the headline numbers are striking, with the caveats right beside them. Polsia claims a ten million dollar annual run rate with no full time staff. It raised a thirty million dollar funding round. But month one churn is about fifty percent, which Ben argues is not a disaster because many people try an idea, learn it is not for them, and leave, and its bill from one model provider reportedly hit between one million and one point five million dollars a month before they worked on fixing it. That cost line is the real lesson. An agent business can look like pure margin until you see what the agents themselves cost to run.
Max: That is why this segment matters for builders. The question is no longer whether agents can do the work. The question is whether the work they do is worth more than the computing bill they create, and whether half your customers staying past month one is enough when the product keeps improving while they sleep. Andrew's closing question was whether ten percent of the companies started on the platform making real money counts as success, and honestly, for a brand new category, it might.
Alex: Time for the one actionable takeaway. Steal the shared move from all three stories today. Build one small engine you can run every day, a packaged service, one search guide, or one agent readiness check, and let repetition, not inspiration, do the compounding. Engines beat launches because engines are still working on day sixty, which, as we learned today, is exactly when the money shows up.
