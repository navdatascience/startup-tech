Max: And the product could not be simpler to explain. You tap record on your phone, and Wave transcribes the conversation and writes a summary. Alex: Let us pull out the playbook, because the building method is the lesson. Step one, he kept every step small enough to understand. He did not ask the AI to build the whole app. He asked how to do the next step, learned it, and moved on. Step two, he started with one screen. Not a platform, not a suite, one screen that recorded and summarized. Step three, he stayed solo. He still does the engineering and the support himself, which means every customer complaint lands directly on the person who can fix it.
Max: And notice what he brought from his old job. He was not a blank slate. He had spent years watching how busy people actually work, so he picked a pain he had lived himself, the hours lost to notes and follow ups after meetings. The AI gave him the building skill he was missing. His experience gave him the problem. That pairing is why this worked, and it is a pattern anyone listening can copy this week.
Alex: Now to our second segment, from Greg Isenberg and the Startup Ideas Podcast. This is his solo episode titled Five Trillion Dollar Opportunity, AI Roll Ups, and the scale here is enormous. Greg says about five trillion dollars worth of small businesses will change hands over the coming years because their owners are retiring. He cites a McKinsey figure that about a million of those businesses could actually sell by twenty thirty five.
Max: These are accounting practices, insurance agencies, property management companies, and support centers. Owners who started in the nineteen eighties or nineteen nineties built loyal client bases over thirty years, and now their kids do not want the business and their employees do not want to run it. Greg points out that big investors are already moving. Thrive Holdings and General Catalyst buy firms like these and run AI agents inside them to lift margins.
Alex: Here is why the timing works, in Greg's telling. Look at what fills the day inside one of these firms. Someone types numbers from a document into a spreadsheet. Someone chases a client for the same missing document for the third time. Someone writes the same status update fifty times a week. Greg says that not long ago, AI pointed at that work produced a rough draft a human had to redo. Now, set up properly, agents can actually finish those tasks.
Max: And here is the part for solo founders, because Greg is clear that the big funds chase bigger deals, and that leaves a small deal gap. His version is a one person holding company. One small firm in one niche. A clear folder structure, written agent files for each recurring job, and one hard rule, a human approves anything that leaves the building. Your week is spent reviewing agent work, talking to clients, and improving the playbook, not typing numbers into spreadsheets.
Alex: On the money side, Greg's model is honest about where the profit comes from. The firm keeps charging its existing clients what they already pay, because those clients bought trust built over decades. The gain is on the cost side, as agents take over the repetitive work and margins rise. And Greg does close with the strongest arguments against roll ups, including the one he takes most seriously, which is that buying a business is not the same as buying software. Clients can leave, and trust does not transfer on paper. So the version one is small, local, and careful.
